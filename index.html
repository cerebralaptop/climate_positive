About the Roadmap

A Shared Vision for a
Climate Positive Future

In 2018, the GBCA released 'A Carbon Positive Roadmap for the built environment'. This landmark document outlined how to decarbonise the built environment, using Green Star to set targets and guidance for the industry.

Since then, industry has adopted these targets, shifting the conversation from should the built environment be net zero to how we achieve this goal. The Climate Positive Roadmap series now spans three key publications covering buildings, net zero ambitions, and precincts.

The roadmaps provide the direction, goals, motivation and pathways that can deliver industry change at scale. They align the industry across a common language, targets, and intent.

The Urgency

Why We Need to Act Now

1.1°C Already

The planet has already warmed 1.1°C above pre-industrial levels. Australia's land areas have warmed around 1.4°C since 1910, with bigger heat waves, floods, and fire seasons.

1.5°C Threshold

We are on a trajectory to exceed 1.5°C within two decades. The IPCC demands strong, rapid, and sustained emissions reductions to net zero, with a focus on eliminating fossil fuels.

Built Environment Impact

Buildings account for approximately 25% of Australia's emissions. The built environment is a key sector for rapid decarbonisation and must transform earlier than most sectors.

“Emissions must be net zero by 2050 at the latest. This can only be done with drastic cuts, and by stopping the use of fossil fuels in buildings and precincts as quickly as possible.”

— IPCC 6th Assessment Report, 2021

The Science is Clear

The IEA's Net Zero by 2050 roadmap identifies electrification and energy efficiency as accounting for nearly 70% of buildings-related emissions reductions through to 2050. The report defines the end state as "zero carbon ready buildings" — highly energy efficient, using fully decarbonised energy supply, and maximising efficient use of materials.

The Financial Case

The Task Force for Climate-related Financial Disclosure (TCFD) recognises transition risks for organisations that fail to decarbonise. Large investors are seeking assets aligned with the Paris Agreement. Demonstrating commitment to keeping warming below 1.5°C is becoming a competitive advantage.

Three Key Barriers to Overcome

Fossil Fuels

Gas in heating, cooking, hot water and power; diesel for backup generation. The IPCC notes that continued installation of fossil fuel infrastructure will likely result in overshooting climate targets.

Phase out from new buildings ASAP; from existing buildings by 2040.

Refrigerants

High-GWP hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs) in HVAC systems. Viable commercial alternatives now exist, including natural refrigerants and hydrofluoro-olefins (HFOs).

Phase out HFCs from new buildings ASAP; from existing by 2040.

Embodied Carbon

Emissions from sourcing, manufacturing, installation, and demolition of materials represent ~20% of a typical building's lifecycle emissions, mostly released on day one.

40% reduction by 2030; zero embodied carbon by 2050.

Precincts & Communities

Climate Positive Precincts

Urban development sets the scene for carbon impacts for generations. Early decisions impact long-term transport, buildings, water, and waste infrastructure emissions.

What Is a Precinct?

A precinct is a unified area of urban land with a clearly defined geographic boundary, synonymous with neighbourhood or district. This can apply to greenfield residential communities, brownfield or infill sites, urban renewal areas, or campuses such as universities, business parks, or airports.

The typical elements of precincts include buildings, public and private open space, streetscapes, transport infrastructure, and utility infrastructure (communications, energy, water, sewerage, and waste).

Why Focus on Precincts?

Precinct developments create powerful opportunities for carbon reductions by linking together planning, urban design, buildings, transport, and infrastructure. Climate positive precincts are efficient, healthy, resilient and tread lightly on our planet.

They also offer developers the opportunity to demonstrate their commitment to sustainability, answering a growing call for action from investors, shareholders and consumers alike.

Precinct Types

Greenfield

New residential developments and town centres built from the ground up.

Brownfield

Urban renewal areas, infill sites, and regeneration of existing areas.

Campus

Universities, industrial parks, business parks, airports, and institutional campuses.

Key Stakeholders

Federal, State & Local Government

Set the context through policy, regulation and strategic planning. Provide approvals and are often recipients of assets.

Infrastructure Authorities & Utilities

Plan, deliver and operate key energy, water, communications, transport and waste infrastructure.

Precinct Developers & Operators

Set the vision and masterplan, enabling construction and operation to deliver on that vision.

Building Developers & Operators

Develop, own, or operate the buildings within the precinct.

Materials Manufacturers

Manufacture and supply materials and products for construction. Must align supply chains for lower carbon.

Professional Services

Planners, urban designers, landscape architects, and engineers playing an important role in planning and design.

Framework

Climate Positive Principles

These principles provide a framework for developing climate positive solutions in the planning, design, construction and operation of precincts and buildings.

Reduce

Built with Lower Upfront Emissions

Materials for construction have low carbon impact. Developers and operators collaborate with the supply chain. Designs optimise efficient material use.

Highly Efficient

Clear emissions reduction targets through latest design and technology. Precinct-wide energy, water, and waste systems encourage a circular economy.

Walkable and Liveable

Transport emissions reduced by good urban design, promotion of active transport, and low carbon options. Walking and cycling encouraged over vehicle use.

Eliminate

Fossil Fuel-Free

No natural gas for stationary energy. No gas reticulation system. Buildings, open spaces, and infrastructure do not use fossil fuel for energy.

Powered by Renewables

All stationary energy from renewable generation — solar PV, wind, and/or hydro power. Generated on-site or procured from off-site sources, paired with energy storage.

Compensate

Offset with Nature

Remaining emissions offset through nature-based solutions that prioritise positive biodiversity outcomes. On-site offsets contribute to amenity, urban cooling and water management; off-site through credible providers.

Precinct Actions

Five Actions for Climate Positive Precincts

These are the key actions all stakeholders must embrace and advocate for to achieve climate positive precincts.

01

Embed Climate Positive Pathways into All Stages of Planning

Planning policies should drive the sustainability and liveability of our cities and precincts. Infrastructure authorities, planners, consultants, and developers must embrace the climate positive framework from the earliest stages.

  • City and regional planning policies must provide clear pathways aligned with climate positive principles
  • Precinct-scale planning and urban design must align with climate positive goals
  • Early infrastructure planning must evaluate and drive low carbon outcomes
  • Knowledge and capacity must be built amongst planners and urban designers
02

Commit to Fossil Fuel-Free Precincts

One of the biggest opportunities is ensuring precincts do not use fossil fuels for energy. Natural gas has no role to play in the built environment for most common uses.

  • Developers must commit to gas-free, all-electric developments
  • Governments must ensure policies do not mandate gas connection
  • Peak bodies and education institutions must promote gas-free futures
  • Green hydrogen may serve specialised industrial uses, not homes
03

Remove Barriers to Low Carbon Precinct Energy Solutions

Precinct-scale energy systems provide local generation and use of clean energy, delay network augmentation, lower costs, and improve grid resilience.

  • Establish clear, efficient approval processes for distributed energy systems
  • Make it easier for developers to play a role in precinct-scale energy solutions
  • Government support for developer-energy industry partnerships
  • Enable microgrids, virtual power plants, and precinct-scale energy trading
04

Drive Lower Upfront Carbon in Materials and Construction

Embodied carbon from materials and construction is significant. The supply chain must collaborate to deliver low carbon materials and reduce construction emissions.

  • Set embodied carbon targets for precinct infrastructure and buildings
  • Adopt standardised measurement approaches and benchmarks
  • Procurement processes must drive demand for low carbon materials
  • Support the Materials & Embodied Carbon Leaders' Alliance (MECLA)
05

Commit to Delivering Low Carbon Buildings in All Precincts

Buildings are the foundation of precincts and must be climate positive. They need to be highly efficient, fossil fuel-free, powered by renewables, with lower embodied emissions.

  • Align with the Climate Positive Roadmap for buildings targets
  • Design guidelines and covenants must require low carbon buildings
  • Support Green Star certification for all major buildings
  • Retrofit existing buildings to be net zero carbon in operations

Definitions

Understanding the Terms

Net Zero

The balance between greenhouse gas produced and removed from the atmosphere on a net annual basis. A technical, open-ended term that must specify focus (e.g., net zero in operations).

Carbon Neutral

Buildings that address all emissions so the carbon account is zero, through energy efficiency, renewables, and offsets (in that order). Measured on a net annual basis. In Australia, defined through the Climate Active program.

Climate Positive

Buildings that address all relevant emissions in line with a 1.5°C trajectory. Fossil fuel-free, highly efficient, powered by renewables, with lower embodied emissions, and remaining emissions offset with nature.

Green Star

The Role of Green Star

Green Star is an internationally recognised rating system that delivers independent verification of sustainable outcomes throughout the life cycle of the built environment.

Green Star Buildings

For new assets in construction, Green Star Buildings requires buildings to be built with lower upfront emissions, be highly efficient, fossil fuel-free, powered by renewables, and offset remaining emissions with nature.

Green Star also addresses transition risks identified by the TCFD, helping manage greenhouse gas emissions, energy use, energy source, upfront carbon, and market reputation at an asset level.

Green Star Performance

For existing buildings in operations, Green Star Performance drives the acceleration of decarbonisation through efficiency improvements, elimination of electricity emissions, compensation for hard-to-abate emissions, and eventual neutralisation of all remaining emissions.

The Future Focus rating tools will extend climate positive requirements to homes, fitouts, precincts, and communities by 2030.

The Role of Offsets

Green Star allows high-quality nature-based offsets for selected emission scopes. The approach is aligned with the Oxford Principles for Net Zero Aligned Carbon Offsetting, which call for shifting to carbon-removal offsetting and long-lived storage.

Eligible offset activities include afforestation, reforestation, improved forest management, agricultural land management, and wetlands restoration. Standards include VCS (Verra), Gold Standard, CCBA, and the Australian ERF.

Nature-based solutions provide dual benefits: they draw down carbon from the atmosphere while also supporting livelihoods, restoring soils, reducing flood risk, sustaining natural resources, and buffering communities from climate impacts.

Over time, users should increase the proportion of offsets from carbon removals rather than emission reductions, ultimately reaching 100% carbon removals by mid-century.
