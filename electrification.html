Practical Guides

Electrification of Buildings

Eliminating fossil fuels from new and existing buildings through proven all-electric technologies. Heat pumps, induction cooking, and renewable energy are the pathway to a decarbonised built environment.

The Case for Electrification

Why Go All-Electric?

Natural gas represents 10–30% of building emissions. The electricity grid is decarbonising rapidly, while the gas network cannot. Electrification is the clear path forward.

10–30% Of building emissions from natural gas
14 Mt CO2 per year from gas in Australian buildings
3:1 Heat pump efficiency vs gas boiler
84× Methane’s warming impact vs CO2 over 20 years

Climate Change

All-electric buildings powered by renewables are the preferred pathway to decarbonise. The IPCC and IEA support electrification as the key solution for buildings. Methane emissions from gas must be reduced far faster than previously assumed.

Investor Benefits

Global sustainable debt capital surpassed US$700 billion in 2021. All-electric buildings access sustainable finance, green bonds, and sustainability-linked loans at lower interest rates. The Climate Bonds Initiative recognises Green Star’s electrification pathway.

Health & Wellbeing

Natural gas combustion generates toxic air pollutants including nitrogen dioxide. Gas cooking increases childhood asthma risk by 42%. All-electric services eliminate these pollutants, improving indoor air quality for all occupants.

“Any building designed today that uses gas will emit far more than a well-designed all-electric building — even if the electric building does not procure renewable electricity.”

— GBCA Practical Guide to Electrification

Electric Alternatives

Technologies for All-Electric Buildings

Proven electric alternatives exist for every common fossil fuel use in buildings. The technology is available today.

Electric Alternative

Heat Pumps for Space Heating

Heat pumps deliver a coefficient of performance (CoP) of approximately 3:1, producing three units of heat for every unit of electricity consumed. They extract heat from the air or ground rather than generating it by combustion, making them vastly more efficient than gas boilers.

Types include air-source heat pumps (most common), ground-source heat pumps (highest efficiency), and water-source heat pumps for larger buildings.

Replaced

Gas Boilers for Space Heating

Gas boilers operate at 80–90% efficiency, converting less of their input energy to useful heat. They produce direct combustion emissions (Scope 1) plus upstream methane emissions (Scope 3). Over 25 years, a gas boiler produces 6.0 tCO2e per MWh of heat vs 0.6–2.2 for heat pumps.

Electric Alternative

Heat Pump Hot Water Systems

Heat pump hot water systems are 3–5 times more efficient than gas hot water. They can also serve as thermal batteries, heating water during off-peak periods when electricity is cheapest and cleanest, then storing it for later use.

Replaced

Gas Hot Water Systems

Gas instantaneous and storage systems are less efficient and emit greenhouse gases directly. Gas hot water is typically the largest single gas use in residential buildings and a significant energy consumer in commercial buildings.

Electric Alternative

Induction Cooktops

Induction cooking is approximately 90% energy efficient, compared to just 40% for gas cooktops. It heats faster, provides precise temperature control, produces zero indoor air pollutants, and is safer as the cooktop surface stays cool. Leading commercial kitchens and residential developments are adopting induction.

Replaced

Gas Cooktops

Gas cooktops are approximately 40% efficient and release nitrogen dioxide, carbon monoxide, and formaldehyde indoors. Studies show gas stoves increase the risk of childhood asthma. They are also a significant barrier to achieving all-electric buildings and accessing renewable energy for all energy needs.

Backup Power

Diesel backup generators can be replaced with battery energy storage systems (BESS) paired with on-site solar. For critical facilities, hydrogen fuel cells are emerging as a zero-emission alternative. Building codes are being updated to recognise these alternatives.

New Buildings

Building All-Electric from Day One

All new buildings can and should be designed all-electric today. The technologies exist, costs are competitive, and there is no reason to install gas infrastructure in new construction.

Design Considerations

Designing an all-electric building from the outset is simpler and often cheaper than retrofitting later. Key considerations include:

  • Sizing electrical infrastructure for all-electric loads from the start
  • Eliminating gas connection fees and infrastructure costs
  • Maximising rooftop solar and battery storage opportunities
  • Specifying high-performance heat pumps for HVAC and hot water
  • Ensuring EV charging readiness in car parks

Cost Considerations

All-electric buildings eliminate several cost items associated with gas:

  • No gas connection or supply charges (saving $400–$900+ per dwelling per year)
  • No gas meter rooms or gas risers freeing up floor area
  • Reduced gas safety compliance and maintenance costs
  • Heat pumps have lower operating costs than gas equivalents
  • Future-proofed against stranded asset risk as gas is phased out

Case Studies — New Buildings

Community Facility • Parramatta, NSW

PHIVE

Parramatta’s new civic building is fully electric with no gas connection. It features rooftop solar, high-performance heat pumps, induction kitchens, and achieved a 6 Star Green Star rating. It demonstrates that major public buildings can be designed all-electric from the start.

Residential • Melbourne, VIC

Nightingale 1

A pioneering all-electric residential building in Brunswick. No gas connection, centralised heat pump hot water, rooftop solar, and 100% renewable electricity procurement. It proved that apartment buildings can be delivered without fossil fuels at competitive cost.

Education • Melbourne, VIC

Gillies Hall, Monash University

A 504-bed all-electric student accommodation building using mass timber construction. Features heat pump HVAC, centralised heat pump hot water, induction cooking, and rooftop solar. Achieved a 6 Star Green Star Design & As Built rating.

Retail • Melbourne, VIC

Burwood Brickworks

The world’s most sustainable shopping centre (Living Building Challenge). All-electric design with no gas infrastructure, rooftop solar, battery storage, rainwater harvesting, and regenerative design principles. Demonstrates all-electric is achievable for retail.

Commercial Office • Canberra, ACT

ACT Government Office

A fully electric government office building achieving high performance through heat pump systems, energy-efficient design, and on-site renewable energy. Shows that government can lead by example in all-electric building delivery.

Existing Buildings

Retrofitting for an All-Electric Future

Most buildings standing today will still be operational in 2050. Electrifying existing buildings now ensures they are future-proofed for a decarbonised world.

Seven Key Issues When Electrifying

Space Heating

Replace gas boilers with air-source or water-source heat pumps. Heat pumps provide both heating and cooling, consolidating systems. Consider staged replacement aligned with equipment end-of-life to manage costs.

Domestic Hot Water

Replace gas hot water systems with heat pump hot water units. Centralised heat pump systems with thermal storage tanks are ideal for multi-tenancy buildings. Heat pump hot water can act as thermal batteries.

Cooking

Transition from gas cooktops to induction. Address tenant concerns through education about induction’s superior performance. In commercial kitchens, induction woks and ranges are now widely available and preferred by many chefs.

Other Gas Systems

Address remaining gas uses: pool heating (use heat pumps), outdoor heaters (use electric radiant heaters), laundry (heat pump dryers), and laboratory gas (evaluate alternatives or plan for last disconnection).

Electric Vehicles

Plan for EV charging infrastructure as part of building electrification. Assess electrical capacity requirements for future EV loads. Smart charging systems can manage peak demand and align with renewable generation.

Electrical Capacity

Assess whether existing electrical infrastructure can accommodate the shift from gas to electric. In many cases, the peak electrical load does not increase significantly because heat pumps are more efficient. Engage with distribution networks early.

Space & Location

Plan where new electric equipment will be located. Heat pumps may require different placement than gas boilers. Consider acoustics for external heat pump units. In many cases, removing gas infrastructure frees up space for electric systems.

Certification

Rating Tools & Frameworks

Major sustainability rating tools and financial frameworks now recognise and reward building electrification.

Green Star

Green Star Buildings and Performance require fossil fuel elimination for the highest ratings. The Climate Positive Pathway mandates all-electric buildings as a core requirement. 6 Star Green Star buildings must be fossil fuel-free.

NABERS

NABERS energy ratings benchmark against grid emission factors that will increasingly favour all-electric buildings as the grid decarbonises. Electrification is the clearest path to higher NABERS Energy ratings over time.

Climate Bonds Initiative

The Climate Bonds Initiative recognises Green Star’s Climate Positive Pathway as an automatic compliance route for green bonds, specifically due to its electrification requirements and energy reduction targets.

GRESB

The Global Real Estate Sustainability Benchmark assesses ESG performance of real estate portfolios. Electrification strategies directly support improved GRESB scores through lower Scope 1 and 2 emissions.

Science Based Targets (SBTi)

The Science Based Targets initiative requires organisations to set emissions reduction targets aligned with the Paris Agreement. Electrification is the most direct pathway to meeting building sector SBTi targets.

EU Taxonomy

The EU Taxonomy for sustainable activities makes clear that highly energy-efficient all-electric buildings comply with its requirements, helping direct investor interest to these assets.

Common Misconceptions

Myths & Facts About Electrification

Myth: “Gas is cheaper to run”

Heat pumps with a CoP of 3 produce three times more heat per unit of energy than a gas boiler. Even where gas prices are low, the efficiency advantage of heat pumps means lower total operating costs. All-electric buildings also eliminate separate gas supply charges.

Myth: “The grid isn’t clean enough yet”

Even on Australia’s most carbon-intensive grid (Victoria), heat pumps already emit less than gas boilers. As the grid rapidly decarbonises towards 82% renewables by 2030, the emissions gap widens dramatically in favour of electrification.

Myth: “Hydrogen will replace gas”

Green hydrogen is extremely energy intensive to produce and is far more efficiently used in heavy industry and transport. The IPCC, IEA, and Australian Government all support direct electrification as the preferred pathway for buildings.

Myth: “Chefs prefer gas cooking”

Induction cooking provides superior temperature control, heats faster, and is 90% energy efficient compared to 40% for gas. Leading commercial kitchens are increasingly adopting induction for its precision, safety, and lower kitchen temperatures.

Myth: “Electric infrastructure can’t handle the load”

Because heat pumps are 3–5 times more efficient than gas, the actual increase in electrical demand is typically modest. Peak demand can often be managed through smart controls, thermal storage, and time-of-use optimisation.

Myth: “It costs too much to retrofit”

When aligned with equipment end-of-life replacement cycles, the marginal cost of electrification is minimal. The long-term savings from lower operating costs, eliminated gas charges, and improved asset value typically deliver a positive return.

Whether you’re designing a new building or planning a retrofit, going all-electric is the smartest investment for a decarbonised future.
