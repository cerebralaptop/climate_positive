Grid & Energy

Grid-Interactive Buildings
& Optimised Precincts

As Australia’s electricity grid transitions to renewables, buildings and precincts must become smart, flexible, and grid-interactive to support clean energy and reduce costs.

The Opportunity

Buildings as Grid Assets

Buildings consume ~50% of Australia’s electricity. During peak periods, they use ~77% of system capacity. Making buildings grid-interactive unlocks enormous potential.

50% Of Australia’s electricity consumed by buildings
77% Of peak capacity used by buildings
$1.7B Annual savings from load shifting
0.6% National emissions cut by demand shifting
“By shifting one third of the load in buildings for just three hours a day, five days a week, we would reduce Australia’s annual emissions by 0.6% (equivalent to 180,000 homes) without decreasing energy use, and reduce electricity supply costs by $1.7 billion each year.”

— GBCA Grid-Interactive Efficient Buildings Discussion Paper

From Net Zero to Zero

Grid-Interactive Efficient Buildings

A grid-interactive efficient building (GEB) has four key characteristics that enable it to support the clean energy transition.

Core Element

Efficient

Highly energy efficient through best-practice design, insulation, glazing, and systems. Efficiency reduces total energy demand, making it easier to power the building with renewables and reducing peak loads on the grid.

Core Element

Electrified

Fully electric with no fossil fuel use. Electrification enables 100% renewable energy supply and unlocks demand flexibility. Heat pumps, induction cooking, and battery storage replace all gas and diesel systems.

Core Element

Smart

Equipped with building management systems, smart meters, sensors, and digital controls that provide real-time visibility of energy use and carbon intensity. Data enables informed decisions about when and how energy is consumed.

Core Element

Flexible

Capable of shifting energy demand in response to grid signals. Uses thermal mass, battery storage, and smart controls to shift loads to times when renewable energy is abundant and cheap, reducing peak demand and supporting grid stability.

How Load Shifting Works

Grid-interactive buildings shift energy use to align with renewable generation. For example, air conditioning systems can pre-cool buildings in the morning when solar generation peaks, reducing the need for energy during the expensive late-afternoon peak. Hot water systems heat water during off-peak periods. Battery systems store midday solar for evening use.

This demand flexibility would effectively provide new peak capacity equivalent to 52% of Australia’s existing coal fleet or more than 100% of all gas and diesel generators.

Benefits of Grid-Interactivity

  • Lower energy costs through time-of-use optimisation
  • Reduced emissions by consuming cleaner energy at the right times
  • Improved grid stability supporting higher renewable penetration
  • Revenue opportunities from demand response and VPP participation
  • Reduced infrastructure investment through avoided peak capacity

Precinct Scale

Grid-Optimised Precincts

All-electric precincts can go further by implementing precinct-scale energy solutions that manage generation, storage, and consumption collectively.

Energy Models for Precincts

Traditional Grid Connection

Each building connects individually to the distribution network. Simple but misses opportunities for precinct-scale optimisation. Still the most common model but being replaced by more sophisticated approaches in new developments.

Embedded Network

A private electricity network within the precinct boundary. The precinct operator acts as an intermediary between the external grid and individual buildings, enabling bulk purchasing, on-site generation sharing, and centralised energy management.

Microgrid

A self-contained energy system with local generation, storage, and load management. Can operate connected to or independent from the main grid. Provides energy resilience, local renewable energy, and the ability to island during grid outages.

Virtual Power Plant (VPP)

Multiple distributed energy resources (solar, batteries, EVs) across the precinct aggregated and coordinated to act as a single dispatchable resource. VPPs can provide services to the broader grid while optimising energy costs within the precinct.

Design Considerations

Peak Demand Management

Design precinct infrastructure to manage and reduce peak electricity demand through smart controls, thermal storage, battery systems, and staggered building loads. This reduces network charges and connection costs.

On-Site Renewables

Maximise solar PV across all available roof and ground areas. Consider community solar farms within the precinct. Pair with battery storage for self-consumption optimisation and grid export management.

EV Infrastructure

Plan for significant EV charging loads from day one. Smart charging systems manage charging times to align with renewable generation and avoid peak demand. Vehicle-to-grid (V2G) technology enables EVs to act as mobile batteries.

Battery Storage

Precinct-scale batteries store excess solar for evening use, provide backup power, and participate in grid services. Community batteries can serve multiple buildings more efficiently than individual systems.

Thermal Storage

Hot water tanks, ice storage, and the building’s own thermal mass can shift heating and cooling loads to off-peak periods. These are often the lowest-cost form of energy storage in buildings.

All-Electric Buildings

Every building in the precinct must be designed all-electric from the start. No gas infrastructure should be installed. This eliminates fossil fuel use and enables full integration with the precinct energy system.

Leading Practice

Case Studies

Grid-Optimised Precincts

Residential • ACT

Ginninderry

A large-scale all-electric residential community in the ACT. No gas infrastructure is provided. Features community-scale solar, battery storage, and an embedded network. Homes are designed to be highly efficient with rooftop solar as standard.

Mixed Use • Sydney, NSW

The YARDS

An all-electric, grid-optimised mixed-use precinct in NSW. Incorporates embedded network infrastructure, precinct-scale battery storage, and smart energy management systems. Designed to achieve a Green Star Communities rating.

Residential • Queensland

Harbour Shores

An all-electric residential community in Queensland featuring no gas connections, community solar, and battery storage. Demonstrates that the all-electric precinct model works across different Australian climates and markets.

Grid-Interactive Buildings

Education • Sydney, NSW

UTS Dr Chau Chak Wing Building

This landmark university building uses advanced building management systems and thermal mass to shift energy loads. It demonstrates how smart controls can optimise energy use in response to real-time grid conditions and renewable availability.

Commercial • Sydney, NSW

Grosvenor Place

A premium commercial tower implementing grid-interactive strategies including demand response participation, smart HVAC controls, and real-time energy optimisation. Reducing peak demand while maintaining tenant comfort.

Commercial • Sydney, NSW

EG Funds Management

Implementing grid-interactive strategies across their commercial portfolio. Using building analytics and smart controls to shift loads, reduce peak demand, and participate in demand response programs.

Cultural • Sydney, NSW

Sydney Opera House

An iconic cultural venue exploring grid-interactive capabilities including demand response, smart building management, and alignment of energy consumption with renewable availability to reduce its carbon footprint.

Policy Recommendations

Key Recommendations for Government

Update Building Codes

Introduce requirements for all-electric solutions for heating, cooking, and hot water in buildings. Account for future EV electrification. Explore requirements for grid-interactive technologies in future updates to the National Construction Code.

Decarbonise the Grid

Continue efforts to reach 82% renewables by 2030. Explore how programs like NABERS can incentivise electrification and grid-interactive solutions. Upgrade utility infrastructure for increased electricity demand and interactive solutions.

Invest in Research & Tools

Undertake research on grid-interactive building opportunities. Develop digital strategies for integrating buildings as distributed energy resource nodes. Pilot digitalisation technologies and support information tools for the industry.

The Future Grid Depends on Smart Buildings

Grid-interactive buildings and optimised precincts are essential infrastructure for Australia’s clean energy transition — reducing costs, stabilising the grid, and accelerating decarbonisation.
