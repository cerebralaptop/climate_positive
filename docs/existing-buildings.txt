Introduction
From flooding in Europe to fires in our own backyard, it has become clear that
climate change isn’t coming – it is upon us.
The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) released the 6th
assessment report in August 2021. The report notes that the changes that we are
seeing are happening with an average increase of 1.1 degrees over pre-industrial
levels. We are also on a trajectory to exceed 1.5ºC over the next two decades.
The effects of this warming will be dangerous, costly, and potentially irreversible.
This discussion paper takes this to heart.
We propose targets to help set the stage to decarbonise existing buildings
between now and 2050. These targets aim to transform the built environment
alongside our targets for new buildings and precincts.

Why focus on existing buildings?
Existing buildings are the largest share of emissions in the built environment. Most
of those emissions come from energy use (electricity, gas, and diesel), with other
emissions occurring from embodied carbon in part due to maintenance and
refurbishment as well as emissions from water and waste.
Existing buildings can decarbonise, but this takes time. Over the next 30 years,
millions of buildings will need to become more energy efficient, swap out
equipment for one that doesn’t use fossil fuels, while at the same time ensuring a
high level of comfort to the occupants.
There’s not much time left. Together though, we can create a climate
positive future.

Building operation and scope
Green Star Performance is a holistic rating tool for buildings. It will consider and provide
distinct pathways for a diverse range of operational arrangements.
The scope of some credits will change based on how the building is operated and
occupied. The approach changes to account for challenges and opportunities that result
from spaces that are operated by an entity separate to the building owner. Depending
on the typology, the building owner may not have access to information for a significant
portion of the building. This means the approach to achieve decarbonisation will need
to change.
•

•

•

Owner occupied, where the building is occupied by the entity that owns it, and has
control over the building operations either through direct management, or via a
building management contract. Examples of these are hospitals, university buildings,
or other social infrastructure projects. If tenants exist, they are a small part of the
overall footprint e.g. a café or small retail store.
Tenant occupied, where the building owner manages (by themselves or via a
building management contract) the common areas of a building, with other spaces
leased to a tenant (or tenants). Commonly referred to as a base-building / tenant
situation. The building owner manages the common areas and shared services,
which account for a significant portion of building impacts. The owner can exert
influence over tenant decisions, but cannot directly control their decisions. Examples
of these are most commercial office buildings, built to rent apartments, or a retail
centre.
Tenant operated, where the building is owned by one entity, but managed by the
entity occupying it. In this situation, the tenant takes control over the operations of
the building, including the vast majority of energy emissions, water use, and waste
generation. The building owner is responsible for the upkeep of the building and
other minor issues, but not responsible for any fitout works.

Energy Use
By 2040, all building operations should be highly energy efficient.
Why is this important?

What are we trying to do?

What would the credit look like?

The IPCC report highlights two critical aspects that the built
environment must pursue to be on a 1.5°C trajectory:
Reducing our energy consumption and switching to
renewable energy. The IEA Report goes further, noting that
we need both to electrify buildings and continue to reduce
our energy consumption, with this last one being the most
important due to the influence it can have on user behaviour
as well.

As the market has developed, and thanks to an increase in
the use of off-site renewables, it can be easy to dismiss the
importance of driving continuing energy efficiency.

The Energy Use credit aims to tackle the issue of a building’s
energy consumption. The credit would have three levels of
achievement (average, good, excellent), each recognising a
distinct level of energy efficiency over a baseline.

As a decarbonisation strategy, few will deliver as many
benefits as reducing energy consumption.

There are also other benefits. Energy Use is the cost to the
occupant and reducing this has a net benefit to making
sustainable buildings more attractive. It also means lower
infrastructure needs, particularly when coupled with a
stronger building fabric, and with other options that will help
stabilise the grid (the goal of the Grid Resilience credit in the
Resilient category, to be developed).

This credit aims to recognise the importance of continuous
energy efficiency. It recognises that energy efficiency is an
ongoing process and encourages buildings to continue
reaching to improve against a set benchmark. This credit
addresses energy consumption, not emissions from energy
consumption. This is important as the building implements
the requirements of Energy Source and removes fossil fuels
from heating, cooking, and hot water.
This credit explicitly addresses energy consumption under
the building owner’s control. Tenancy energy consumption
from tenant equipment is not considered in this credit.
Emissions from tenants will be under the Tenant Energy
Source and Other Tenant Emissions credit. A sector specific
Tenant Energy Use credit may be released in future.

There would be four pathways in the credit used to set and
measure benchmarks, which must be followed in order. That is,
Pathway B can only followed if Pathway A does not apply. The
pathways are expected to be based on the current Green Star –
Performance ‘Greenhouse Gas Emissions’ credit, but modified
to account for energy consumption. They are:

a.

NABERS Energy rating

b.

Commercial building energy disclosure baseline report
(soon to be updated by the Federal Government).

c.

Comparison against peers.

d.

Comparison against a historical baseline.

GBCA is working with the industrial sector to consider a
prescriptive pathway for tenant operated buildings.

Embodied Carbon Emissions
By 2040, all buildings should only use sources of energy that are fully decarbonised.
Why is this important?

What are we trying to do?

What would the credit look like?

The built environment is a major consumer of global
resources, with estimates stating that 50% of all materials
are used in buildings. While most of these are due to new
building construction, building maintenance and
refurbishments are included in this number.

To successfully drive down embodied carbon emissions
during operations, this credit aims to create a practice of
considering embodied carbon in a procurement setting. It
also aims to recognize embodied carbon reductions across
significant refurbishment or de-fit activities. The credit would
also aim to recognize extending the life of a building’s major
components where possible.

The credit would have three parts:

The carbon emissions from these materials, referred to as
embodied carbon, have largely been overlooked. In a
building’s emission profile, the emissions from use,
maintenance, and refurbishment activities emissions are
around 5 to 10% of all its emissions. As the energy sources
become cleaner, the proportion of emissions from these
activities becomes significant. As buildings are refurbished,
to extend their life, the impact from these activities will be
significant, with embodied carbon emissions being 85% of
all remaining emissions in 2050 if no action is taken.

Other Carbon Emissions
By 2035, all building owners should compensate for remaining emissions with carbon removal activities
Why is this important?

What are we trying to do?

What would the credit look like?

Once the emissions from energy and materials are taken
into account, the emissions from refrigerants and other
emissions under the building owners’ control become an
issue of concern.

This credit is aiming to create incentives to ensure the
building or asset owner take responsibility for emissions
which combined are a meaningful portion of the building’s
lifetime emissions. The credit aims to do this, by placing a
specific cost to these emissions by requiring offsetting of
these on an ongoing basis and ensuring that they are driven
down over time.

The credit would have two parts:

The impact of refrigerant emissions on climate change has
not been properly quantified in buildings, but globally,
phasing down refrigerants with a high global warming
potential could avoid up to 0.4C of global warming by 2100.
In 2019, the world agreed to the Kigali accord, which aims to
phase out these refrigerants. This means buildings will need
to be upgraded to account for them. However, 90% of
emissions from refrigerants happen either through leakage
or at the end of life.
Other carbon emissions from buildings are also under the
building owner’s control in most situations. Emissions from
water consumption and waste generation and disposal can
be heavily influenced by decisions made by the owner.
These can account to be around 5% of the building’s total
lifetime emissions. Remaining embodied carbon emissions
also need to be addressed.

Targets over time
Other Carbon Emissions

The credit recognises that there is an operational aspect to
all these emissions, but also that there is an opportunity for
improvements over time as plant and equipment is replaced.
In the case of refrigerants, it would place additional
emphasis on the drawdown and disposal of any refrigerants,
and emphasise that replacements must be lower GWP.
The credit works well alongside other credits such as
Energy Source. That credit’s requirement for a zero carbon
action plan would include actions related to refrigerants and
other emissions.
This credit explicitly addresses emissions under the building
owner’s control. Emissions that form part of a tenancy is
addressed by the Other Tenant Emissions credit.

What would the credit look like?

One of the largest sources of emissions in the built
environment is tenant emissions. These emissions are
considered Scope 3 emissions, that is ancillary emissions,
and are usually beyond the control of the building owner.
However, the building owner does have the capacity to
influence a tenant’s decisions.

This credit aims to encourage influencing tenants to procure
renewable energy or offset their emissions.

This credit aims to ensure the building owner to proactively
attempt to influence the tenant to procure renewables if they
aren’t yet doing so.

Most tenant’s energy use is limited to equipment and
cooking activities. The energy use is partly limited by
availability of equipment in the market, age of equipment in
the tenancy, and user behaviour. As these are difficult to
significantly change by the building owner, this means that
the procurement of energy by the tenant is the biggest lever
available to significantly reduce their emissions.

Targets over time

Moving tenants away from using gas, in most cases in
cooking situations, or hot water in the case of apartments,
can be done by the base building owner not providing gas in
the building. However, this may take time, so until then, the
best strategy is to work with tenants to shift their equipment
away from gas and into electricity. In the vast majority of
situations, the technology exists for this to occur.
The other aspect is the source of electricity. Some tenants
already have procurement plans to move into renewable
electricity. Other tenants have not done this due to a lack of
capability or knowledge that this is possible. Having the
building owner provide a path for these tenants to access
renewable electricity through any number of mechanisms,
would allow these tenants to change their source of energy
to renewables.

As noted in Embodied Carbon Emissions, emissions from
products and materials are a significant contributor to
climate change. While the focus around embodied carbon
emissions tends to be on structural materials, as they are
the largest source of emissions, the impact from churn in
fitouts should not be underestimated.

This credit aims to encourage building owners to work with
tenants to account, reduce and compensate any emissions
from fitout works.

This credit aims to ensure the building owner proactively
influences the tenant to reduce and compensate for their
emissions from fitout works.

In the vast majority of cases the building owner has some
capacity to influence the direction of fitouts in their building.
They may also have more expertise than the tenant, helping
them reduce their emission from a new fitout, and to help
them compensate for any emissions through a well
structured program. The building owner may choose to do
this by providing options, encouraging a previous tenant to
pass on their fitout, etc.

The credit would have two components:

In a typical building, as with emissions from operations, the
emissions from fitout churn can be a quarter to half of the
building’s lifetime embodied carbon emissions. In a typical
tenanted building, it is likely that a tenancy will be
refurbished at least once within a 5 to 7 year period. This
churn of removing the existing fitout, making good the
space, and reintroducing new fitout elements generates
significant emissions which are rarely quantified.

Targets over time

The purpose overall, is to ensure the emissions from fitouts
are being considered at all times, and to encourage
measures to be taken to reduce their impact.

The next steps in
decarbonising our built environment
Over the next 20 years we will have to decarbonise all existing buildings in Australia.
We also need to take further steps to address remaining emissions and maximise opportunities to take action.

Grid resilience

Renewables matching

Carbon negative existing buildings

Energy demand combined with increased variable
renewable electrify generation as well as
infrastructure constraints can place stress on the
grid as it decarbonises if not planned well.

The next version of Green Star Performance will
strongly encourage the procurement of renewables
on a net annual basis. As the grid decarbonises, it
will be important to also match renewable
generation to actual use.

Removing carbon from the atmosphere has been highlighted as a
key action that must be taken to ensure we can limit warming to
1.5C. There is no pathway available that can get us there without
explicit emission cuts, and explicit emission removals.

Buildings consume over 50% of all electricity in
Australia. As such, buildings must play a part to
address these impacts by managing their loads
dynamically. This can be done through energy
efficiency, on-site photovoltaics, energy storage
solutions, or demand shifting responses. With
proper communication and controls, buildings can
use electricity at optimum times, and with storage
solutions, the consumption can be shifted to times
where there’s peak load.

Over time, we expect a move towards matching
supply on an hourly basis, or even over 15 minutes
intervals. Currently, matching renewables to grid is
difficult as there is no good, easily available
mechanism to do this in real time. Buyers of
renewable power will also face increased costs if
they moved to a high threshold of matching
generation and consumption.

These solutions can increase the reliability and
resiliency of the grid, and can also be used to meet
occupant comfort and other service requirements.

Strategies to manage this issue will include better
procurement mechanisms to include requirements
for higher levels of matching. But it can also include
storage and control solutions to shift use at the
time of generation.

Green Star Performance will include a Grid
Resilience credit to address this issue.

The issue of renewables matching will be the
subject of an upcoming thought leadership paper.

While the rest of this paper is focused on driving the emissions
profile of a building to zero for future emissions, there is still the need
to consider how the built environment should take responsibility for
its historic emissions. Already we are seeing other companies take
responsibilities and targets for historic emissions (Microsoft, Google,
Velux), so one can expect similar strategies to show up over time for
this sector.
The goal of pushing for this strategy is to provide a meaningful
pathway for the built environment to invest in carbon removals,
beyond its current obligations. It can be easy to foresee a future
where building portfolios will be required to calculate their carbon
debt, with a carbon removals strategy created and attached to the
building, akin to a carbon passport in the building’s balance sheet. In
the future, buildings would be able to report a carbon debt of zero,
making them more attractive investments.

The concept of carbon negative existing buildings will be explored in a
leadership challenge for Green Star Performance called Carbon debt.
